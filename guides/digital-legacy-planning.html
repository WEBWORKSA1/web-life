Digital legacy planning

Domains expire, accounts lock and websites go dark when the only person who knows the logins can't act. A digital legacy plan takes an afternoon and protects years of work.

By Web.Life EditorialUpdated 26 November 20264 min read

Why websites are especially vulnerable

A domain name is registered for a fixed term and must be renewed. Hosting is a subscription. If payments stop and nobody else can access the accounts, the site goes offline and, after the registry's grace and redemption periods, the domain can be released for anyone to register. Email on that domain stops working too, which can lock people out of other accounts that send password resets there. For a business, that can mean lost income; for a family, the loss of photos, writing and records.

Step 1: make an inventory

List every digital asset someone might need to find. Use the digital legacy planner, which keeps everything in your browser until you print or export it.

  • Domains: registrar, expiry date, account email.
  • Websites: host, platform, where backups are stored.
  • Email accounts and which domains they use.
  • Income accounts: ad networks, affiliate programmes, payment processors.
  • Cloud storage for photos and documents.
  • Social profiles and communities you run.
  • Your password manager, which ideally holds the credentials for all of the above.

Step 2: decide what should happen

For each item, choose one: keep it running, transfer it to someone, sell it, archive and close it, or simply close it. A profitable website might be sold; a family blog might be archived; a personal social profile might be memorialised or deleted. Write the decision next to each line, with the name of the person who should handle it.

Step 3: give someone a way in

  • Password manager emergency access. Many password managers let you nominate an emergency contact who can request access after a waiting period you set.
  • Platform legacy features. Several major services let you choose what happens after long inactivity or death, such as Google's Inactive Account Manager, Apple's Legacy Contact and Facebook's legacy contact. Set them up and note them in your plan.
  • Registrar and host contacts. Some providers allow additional authorised contacts on an account. Where they do, consider adding a trusted person.

Don't write passwords into documents. Your plan should say where things are and who should act. Keep the actual credentials in a password manager, and tell your trusted contact how emergency access works.

Step 4: reduce the urgency

  • Register important domains for several years and turn on auto-renew.
  • Keep a payment method on file that won't expire soon, and update it when it does.
  • Store backups somewhere your contact can reach, not only inside the hosting account.

Step 5: make it official

A digital inventory is practical, but it isn't a legal document. Mention your digital assets, and where your instructions are kept, in your will or estate plan. In the United States, most states have adopted a version of the Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act, which governs how executors can access online accounts; other countries have their own rules. A qualified estate professional can tell you what applies where you live.

Review once a year

Pick a date, such as your biggest domain's renewal, and review the plan annually. Add new sites and accounts, remove closed ones, and confirm your trusted contacts still agree to help. It's an afternoon of work that turns a potential crisis into a checklist.
