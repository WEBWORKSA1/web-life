How websites are valued and sold

Websites are bought and sold like small businesses. Buyers pay for proven, transferable profit, and they discount anything that looks risky. Knowing how they think helps you build a more valuable site even if you never sell.

By Web.Life EditorialUpdated 26 November 20264 min read

The basic formula

Most small content sites, tools and online businesses are priced as a multiple of average monthly net profit, usually measured over the last six to twelve months. Net profit means revenue minus every cost of running the site, including hosting, software, freelance writers and paid traffic. Multiples quoted across the market commonly fall somewhere between roughly 20 and 50 times monthly profit, with the exact figure depending on risk, growth and how easily the site can be run by someone new. Larger businesses are often valued on annual earnings instead.

Example: a site averaging $2,000 a month in net profit, valued at 30×, is worth about $60,000. Try your own numbers in the valuation estimator.

What raises the multiple

  • History. Two or more years of stable or growing profit.
  • Diversified traffic. Visitors from search, email, social and direct, rather than one source.
  • Diversified revenue. Several income streams, ideally including recurring revenue.
  • Low owner involvement. Documented processes and contractors who can continue.
  • Clean records. Profit and loss statements that match payment processor and bank records, and analytics access for the buyer.
  • Transferable assets. Domain, content, email list, social accounts and supplier relationships that can legally and practically move to a new owner.

What lowers it

  • A recent traffic drop, especially after a search algorithm update.
  • Dependence on a single affiliate programme, ad network or platform that could change terms.
  • A personal brand at the centre of the business: if readers come for you, they may not stay for the buyer.
  • Unclear rights to content or images, or policy violations an ad network could act on.
  • Messy finances, or revenue that can't be verified.

The sale process

  1. Prepare. Gather 12–24 months of profit and loss records, analytics access, a list of assets and a short operating manual.
  2. Value. Get a professional valuation or compare recent sales of similar sites.
  3. Choose a channel. Options include brokers (who vet buyers and handle negotiation for a commission), marketplaces (wider reach, more work for you) and private sales to people you know in your niche.
  4. Due diligence. Serious buyers verify traffic, revenue, costs and ownership. Expect read-only access requests to analytics and payment dashboards. Never hand over full login credentials before the deal is secured.
  5. Agree terms. Price, payment structure (all upfront, or part paid later based on performance), what's included and how long you'll help with the transition.
  6. Use escrow. A reputable escrow service holds the buyer's payment until assets are transferred, protecting both sides.
  7. Transfer. Move the domain, hosting, content, accounts and documentation in an agreed order, then support the buyer as promised.

Red flags when you're the buyer

  • Traffic or revenue that can't be verified directly in the source dashboards.
  • Sudden spikes just before listing.
  • Sellers who resist escrow or push for a fast close.
  • Backlink profiles full of paid or irrelevant links.

Build for value from day one

Every habit that makes a site easier to sell also makes it easier to run: clean records, documented processes, diversified traffic and income, and a single place listing every account.
