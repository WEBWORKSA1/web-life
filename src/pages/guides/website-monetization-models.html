Nine ways websites make money

There are nine common ways websites earn money. The best sites combine two or three that suit their audience's intent. Here's how each works and what the numbers look like.

By Web.Life EditorialUpdated 26 November 20264 min read

Start with intent, not with the model

Ask what your visitors are trying to do when they land on your site. Learning? Comparing options? Solving an urgent problem? Supporting a cause? Each intent pays best through a different model. A recipe reader is a good fit for display ads; someone comparing project management software is a good fit for an affiliate link; a homeowner with a leaking roof is a good fit for a lead form. The examples below use round numbers to show the mechanics; plug your own into the revenue estimator.

1. Display advertising

Ad networks place ads on your pages and pay you based on views and clicks, summarised as RPM (revenue per thousand pageviews). Example: 50,000 pageviews a month at a $6 RPM earns about $300. It suits informational content with lots of pageviews and works passively, but income depends on traffic volume and season. Policies are strict: original content, no encouraging clicks, clear separation between ads and content.

2. Affiliate marketing

You recommend products and earn a commission when a reader buys through your link. Example: 20,000 sessions, 4% click through, 5% of those buy, $25 commission: about $1,000. It suits comparisons, reviews and "best for" pages. Trust is everything: disclose relationships clearly and only recommend what you'd stand behind.

3. Lead generation

You capture enquiries from people who need a service and deliver them to a business, either your own or a partner who pays per lead. For context, published 2026 Google Ads benchmarks put the average cost per lead around $70, which is roughly what businesses already pay to find customers through search ads. A site that sends qualified, consented enquiries can command a share of that. Example: 20,000 sessions × 0.5% conversion × $30 per lead = $3,000. It suits problem-solving content in services such as home improvement, legal, finance and web development.

4. Digital products

Templates, e-books, courses, presets, printables. You create once and sell many times. Example: 40 sales a month at $29 with minimal costs ≈ $1,100. Margins are high, but you need an audience that trusts you and a product that solves a specific problem.

5. Services and consulting

Your site demonstrates expertise; visitors hire you. Even a few clients a month can out-earn every other model on a small site. It doesn't scale without hiring, and it requires your time, which lowers the site's value if you ever sell.

6. Memberships and subscriptions

Readers pay monthly for premium content, community access or tools. Example: 150 members at $8 a month = $1,200 in recurring revenue. Recurring income is highly valued by buyers, but members expect a steady flow of value, and churn is constant.

7. Sponsorships and direct ads

A company pays a flat fee to sponsor a newsletter issue, a tool or a section of your site. Rates depend on how precisely your audience matches what the sponsor sells. A small, focused audience can command more than a large, general one. Always label sponsored content.

8. Donations and supporters

Readers fund work they value. This works best when the site is clearly useful, free and mission-driven, and when the ask says exactly what the money pays for. It's rarely a primary income, but it creates committed fans.

9. Tools and software

Free tools attract visitors and links; a premium tier or sponsored placement monetises them. Tools are among the most linkable content on the web, which also helps every other page rank.

Measure every stream

Tag your campaign links with the UTM builder, track which pages produce revenue (not just traffic), and review the numbers monthly. Diversified, documented income makes a site more resilient and, if you ever sell, more valuable; see how websites are valued.
