SEO fundamentals for new websites

Search engine optimisation isn't a trick. It's making sure search engines can find your pages, understand them, and trust that they answer the question better than the alternatives.

By Web.Life EditorialUpdated 26 November 20264 min read

How search works in three steps

Search engines do three things. They crawl: automated programs follow links and sitemaps to discover pages. They index: they read each page, work out what it's about and store it. And they rank: when someone searches, they choose the pages most likely to satisfy that particular person, weighing relevance, quality, usability and many other signals. A page can't rank if it hasn't been crawled and indexed, so the fundamentals start there.

Step 1: make sure you can be found

  • Verify your site in Google Search Console (and Bing Webmaster Tools). It's free and shows exactly which pages are indexed, which aren't, and why.
  • Submit an XML sitemap listing the pages you want indexed. The robots and sitemap builder can make one.
  • Check robots.txt and noindex tags. A leftover "discourage search engines" setting from development is one of the most common reasons new sites never appear.
  • Link to every important page from somewhere on your site. Orphan pages with no internal links are hard to discover and look unimportant.

Step 2: make each page easy to understand

  • One page, one topic. Decide the main question each page answers and keep it focused.
  • A descriptive title tag that uses the words people search for, near the start. Make every title unique.
  • A meta description that summarises what the reader gets. It doesn't directly affect ranking, but it affects whether people click.
  • Clear headings (one H1, then H2s for sections) so both people and machines can scan the structure.
  • Descriptive link text and image alt text. "Download the launch checklist" beats "click here".
  • Structured data where it fits, generated with the schema generator.

Step 3: deserve the ranking

Google's own guidance centres on helpful, reliable, people-first content: content created to help a reader, not to manipulate rankings. In practice that means:

  • Answering the question fully, with specifics, examples and numbers a generic page wouldn't have.
  • Showing who wrote it and why they know, especially for topics that affect money, health or safety.
  • Keeping facts current and dating your updates.
  • Avoiding thin pages: dozens of near-identical pages targeting slight keyword variations tend to hurt more than help.

Spam policies are real. Hidden text, link schemes, mass-produced pages with little value and scraped content can get pages demoted or removed. Read Google's Search Essentials once; it's short and specific.

Step 4: make it fast and usable

Search engines measure real-user experience. Pages that load slowly, jump around while loading or respond sluggishly lose visitors, and page experience is part of how results are ranked. See Core Web Vitals in plain English for the three metrics and how to fix them. Mobile matters most: Google primarily uses the mobile version of your pages for indexing and ranking.

Step 5: earn links and mentions

Links from relevant, respected sites remain a strong signal that a page is worth trusting. The sustainable ways to earn them are unglamorous: publish something genuinely useful (original data, a free tool, a definitive guide), tell the people who would benefit, and build relationships in your field. Avoid buying links or joining link exchanges; they violate search policies and the risk outweighs the reward.

Your first-month SEO checklist

  1. Verify Search Console and submit your sitemap.
  2. Fix any "not indexed" pages that should be indexed.
  3. Write unique titles and descriptions for every page.
  4. Add internal links from your homepage and navigation to your key pages.
  5. Publish one genuinely useful page per week and link it from an older page.
  6. Check the Performance report each month: which queries show your pages, and which pages are close to page one.

How long it takes

New sites often see their first search impressions within weeks, but meaningful traffic usually takes months of steady publishing. The pages that rank first tend to be specific answers to specific questions. Start there, measure in Search Console, and double down on what gets impressions.
