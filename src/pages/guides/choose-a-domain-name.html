How to choose a domain name that lasts

Your domain is the one part of your website you can't easily change. A good one is short, easy to say, easy to spell and legally safe. Here's how to find it.

By Web.Life EditorialUpdated 26 November 20264 min read

What a domain name actually is

A domain name is a human-friendly address that points to the servers hosting your website and email. You don't buy it outright; you register the right to use it for a period, typically one to ten years, through a registrar. As long as you renew, it's yours to use. If you don't, it eventually becomes available to anyone. That's why the account you register it in, and the renewal settings, matter as much as the name itself.

Five qualities of a lasting name

  1. Short. Fewer characters mean fewer typos and an easier time on business cards, social profiles and podcasts. Aim for under 15 characters before the extension; under 10 is better.
  2. Easy to say. Use the radio test: say the name to someone who hasn't seen it and ask them to type it. If they ask "with a hyphen?" or "the number or the word?", keep looking.
  3. Easy to spell. Avoid creative misspellings, doubled letters at word boundaries (like "expertstrategy") and words people commonly misspell.
  4. Broad enough to grow. A name tied to one product, one city or one year may box you in. Choose something that still fits if your offer expands.
  5. Yours to use. No conflict with an existing trademark in your field, and ideally matching social handles.

Choosing an extension

The extension (the part after the dot) is called a top-level domain, or TLD. .com is still the default many people type from memory, which gives it an advantage for brands that rely on word of mouth. Country-code extensions such as .ca or .co.uk signal a local audience and can suit businesses that only serve one country. Newer generic extensions such as .life, .studio or .app can make a short, descriptive name possible when the .com is taken, and work well when the full name reads naturally.

Two cautions: some extensions have registration requirements (for example, residency for certain country codes), and some carry much higher renewal prices than their first-year price. Check both before you commit.

Check trademarks before you pay

Registering a domain doesn't give you rights to a name if someone else already holds a trademark for it in a related field. Before buying, search:

  • Your national trademark office's database (for example, the USPTO in the United States or CIPO in Canada).
  • The WIPO Global Brand Database for international marks.
  • A plain web search for the name and close variations in your industry.

If an established business uses a similar name for similar goods or services, choose something else. Domain disputes can be costly and, under policies like the UDRP, can end with the domain being transferred. This guide isn't legal advice; for anything borderline, ask a trademark professional.

Generating ideas when your first choice is taken

  • Add a short, natural word: get, try, hq, studio, works, lab.
  • Blend two words where they share letters, so the result still reads cleanly.
  • Use a different extension that forms a natural phrase with the name.
  • Coin a word that is easy to say and has no conflicting meaning in your main markets.

Premium and aftermarket domains

Some names are listed by registries at premium prices, or owned by investors who will sell them. A premium name can be worth it if it saves years of brand-building, but check two things: whether the renewal price is also premium, and whether the seller uses a reputable escrow service for the transfer. Never send payment directly to an unknown seller.

Register it safely

  1. Use a registrar account with a strong, unique password and two-factor authentication.
  2. Register for at least two years and switch on auto-renew with a payment card that won't expire soon.
  3. Turn on registrar lock (transfer lock) to prevent unauthorised transfers.
  4. Use an email address for the account that is not on the domain itself; otherwise, if the domain lapses, you lose the email you'd need to recover it.
  5. Note the registrar and expiry date in your digital legacy plan.

The one-minute final check

Before you click buy, write the full address on a sticky note and read it from across the room. Is it clear? Is there an unfortunate word hiding where two words meet? Would you be happy to say it a thousand times? If yes, register it, and start on your 30-day launch plan.
